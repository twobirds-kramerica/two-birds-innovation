## 1. What this is

A high-frequency, bite-size organic video series where Aaron shows small, real, practical things he has actually built or done with everyday AI and automation. Problem first, cool result second, then a short taste of "how I did it." Humble, playful, curious. A hack showing his work, not a guru lecturing.

Strategic fit: Track C posture (point the machine at distribution). The series is top-of-funnel for Aaron's site and the paid 1-hour **AI Workshops** (never "webinars").

**Positioning guardrails (hard, from canon):**
- Aaron is a 15-year operations and service-delivery leader. AI is the tool, never the identity.
- Never "AI expert / AI consultant / AI practitioner / architect" in any caption, bio, on-screen text, or hashtag copy.
- Voice: curious innovator, humble show-and-tell. "I don't just advise, I build it and it ships."
- No hard-expertise authority claims (medical, legal, financial). He shows handy things he made.
- Every claim passes the 3AM test.

## 2. Competitive research (grounded live, 2026-07-25)

Method: live WebSearch across the TikTok / Instagram / YouTube Shorts / LinkedIn practical-AI content ecosystem, 2026-07-25. Training knowledge not relied on. This is a scan of the landscape shape, not an exhaustive census of every account.

### 2.1 Who is already in the space

| Lane | Representative players (verified live) | Format and positioning |
|---|---|---|
| AI news and tool roundups | Matt Wolfe (YouTube, FutureTools), The Rundown / Rowan Cheung, Superhuman newsletter (Zain Kahn, 790K+ LinkedIn followers, 1.5M newsletter subs) | Weekly news digests, "5 tools you need" listicles. Tool-first, volume-driven, heavily monetized via sponsors and affiliates. |
| Vibe-coding / builder-flex | Riley Brown (@rileybrown.ai, ~640K TikTok, raised $9M for a vibe-code startup) | "I built an app in 2 minutes" demos. Aspirational startup-builder energy, product-first. First mover on AI-coding short form. |

| Short-form craft educators | Kane Kallaway (~550K followers, WavyStudios, hook-archetype frameworks) | Meta-content about making content. Useful for FORMAT craft, not a competitor for Aaron's subject matter. |
| Smart-home / family-tech virality | @rhythmlessman (Alexa catches teen sneaking in, 20M+ views), What's Inside home tours, dad-content accounts | One-off viral family moments. Device automation, not AI agents. Proves the family/household angle has huge mainstream appetite. |
| Prompt-hack accounts | @brain_racked and similar "ChatGPT tips" accounts | Screenshot-and-caption prompt tricks. High volume, low trust, rarely shows a real personal problem being solved. |

### 2.2 What works in the niche (patterns to adopt)

- **Result shown in the first 1 to 2 seconds.** Every high-performing account leads with the payoff, never the setup. Kallaway's whole hook discipline confirms this.
- **Consistency beats volume.** Platform guidance and creator practice converge on 3 to 5 posts/week sustained, rather than daily bursts.
- **One recognizable format repeated.** Riley Brown and Kallaway both run template-driven episodes; the repetition IS the brand.
- **Personality carries trust.** The accounts that endure are people, not tool feeds.

### 2.3 What is saturated (do not compete here)

- AI news recaps and "this new tool just dropped" content. Owned by full-time media operations with sponsor pipelines.
- Generic prompt-tip listicles. Zero differentiation available.
- Guru/authority positioning ("I will 10x your business with AI"). Also directly banned by canon.
- Startup-flex vibe coding ("I built an app, invest in me"). Riley Brown owns it.

### 2.4 Where Aaron's angle is genuinely differentiated

1. **Problem-first, not tool-first.** Nearly all niche content leads with the tool. Aaron leads with "my wife was unhappy about the untrained dog." That is rare and it is the most relatable possible frame.
2. **Everything is real and already shipped.** No affiliate agenda, no sponsored tool of the week. The library IS his actual life and ops stack. Verifiable, and canon-compliant.
3. **Family and household operations run by AI agents** is nearly empty territory. The viral smart-home content proves the appetite, but it is device automation (locks, lights, Alexa). Nobody visible is showing a photographed school calendar turning into shared family calendar events, or an overnight agent doing the boring work. That gap is Aaron's.
4. **The humble-hack persona.** The comedy sign-off and "look what I can do" framing is the exact opposite of the saturated guru tone. It is a moat because gurus cannot copy it without breaking their own positioning.
5. **A mid-career operations leader, not a 24-year-old founder.** The audience segment that most needs practical AI (parents, operators, non-developers, 35 to 60) is under-served by creators who look and live like them.

**Verdict: proceed.** The format lane (short, template-driven, result-first) is proven; the positioning lane (humble, personal, problem-first, family-ops) is open.

## 3. Series format spec (locked, pending Aaron's length ratification)

### 3.1 Named formats

- **QUICK LOOK (default): 45 seconds, hard ceiling 60.** Every episode ships as a Quick Look. Bite-size per Aaron's lean; survives TikTok/Reels/Shorts retention mechanics.
- **LONGER LOOK: up to 90 seconds.** Reserved for episodes where the "how" genuinely needs the room (meta-builder episodes). Never the default; an episode must earn 90 seconds.
- No 30-second named format. 30s is just a tight Quick Look; naming it would create drift pressure.

Recommendation strength: high confidence on Quick Look as default; Aaron ratifies the set (open question, section 12).

### 3.2 Episode template (the 6 beats, with timing for a 45s Quick Look)

| Beat | Time | Content | Convention |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Hook | 0 to 3s | "Look what I can do" line + the end result shown immediately (the Telegram ping arriving, the calendar filling itself, the dashboard) | Result on screen before Aaron's face. Hook line from the hook library (section 5). |
| 2. Problem | 3 to 12s | The everyday pain, plain language, first person | No jargon. The problem must be relatable to a non-technical viewer. |
| 3. Result / show-and-tell | 12 to 25s | What it looks like working, high level | Screen recording or phone-filmed screen. Privacy-scrubbed (section 3.5). |
| 4. How I did it | 25 to 38s | Backwards from result to method, a taste not a tutorial | Formula: "I told [tool] to [outcome]. It [did the thing]. Took me about [honest time]." |
| 5. Sign-off | 38 to 42s | The "look what I can do" signature clip (section 4.3) | Same clip family every episode. The brand stamp. |
| 6. CTA | 42 to 45s | End card: "Full how-to on the site" + AI Workshop booking mention | Every episode backlinks. One CTA, not three. |

### 3.3 Production rules

- **No branded intro/sizzle at the top.** Cold open on the result; an intro costs the first 2 seconds, which are the whole game. The sign-off clip at the END is the branding. (This resolves the brief's sizzle question: sizzle no, sign-off yes.)
- **On-screen text:** captions always on (sound-off viewing); one text overlay style per the visual identity (section 4.4); theme tag shown as a small corner label (e.g. "FAMILY OPS No. 4").
- **Shot inventory per episode:** 1 talking-head or voiceover track, 1 to 3 screen recordings, 1 end card. Deliberately cheap to produce; the pipeline must sustain 3 or more episodes/week.
- **Platform order:** TikTok first, then Instagram Reels, then YouTube Shorts (same vertical master file for all three), LinkedIn for selected episodes only (work-efficiency and meta-builder themes; family content stays off LinkedIn).
- **Cadence target:** 3 episodes/week to start (matches researched best practice; sustainable from the seed backlog depth).

### 3.4 Tier / track rule

Start with a single beginner-friendly track. No "advanced" labelling at launch. If an intermediate track emerges later, label it by theme depth ("Machine Shop" episodes are naturally the deeper track), never with words like "advanced" or "masterclass."

### 3.5 Privacy rule (hard)

Strip kids' names, faces, school names, addresses, real inbox contents, financials, and any client data from anything shown. Re-create demo data where needed and say so on the site detail page ("data shown is recreated"). Mechanism, not private content.

## 4. Branding

### 4.1 Name: "Look What I Can Do" — rights check result

Live check performed 2026-07-25:

- The phrase is the catchphrase of the character Stuart Larkin (Michael McDonald) from **MADtv**, seasons 4 to 12. Note: the brief remembered it as a British comedy bit; it is American (MADtv, 1995 to 2016, currently a CBS/Paramount property). The tone-reference intent is unaffected.
- Web and trademark-aggregator searches surfaced **no live registered trademark** on "Look What I Can Do" for content/entertainment services. It is a common English phrase (also a 1971 children's book title, and countless organic uses), which makes exclusive encumbrance unlikely and equally means Aaron cannot exclusively own it either.
- **Caveat honestly stated:** a formal USPTO TESS and CIPO (Canadian) register query could not be completed through available tooling (database front-ends block automated fetch). The finding is "no evidence of encumbrance," not "cleared by counsel." Before any real branding SPEND (paid design, merch, registration), a 10-minute manual TESS + CIPO search should be done. Filed as part of the Aaron decision action.
- **Clip risk is copyright, not trademark, and it is real.** Using actual MADtv footage as the sign-off would be reuse of Paramount-owned clips inside monetized brand content; platforms' Content ID and rights-holder takedowns make this a recurring operational risk regardless of a fair-use parody argument. **Recommendation: never use original footage. Aaron performs his own 2 to 4 second homage sign-off** (his own costume-free, self-aware version of the bit). An original performance referencing a catchphrase style is standard parody/homage practice and carries no clip-matching risk.

**Recommendation: keep the name.** Contingency names if Aaron wants zero shared history with the sketch (present, do not pick silently): "Watch This" ... "Neat Trick" ... "Little Wins" (bilingual-friendly: "Petites victoires / Little Wins"). All three keep the humble show-and-tell spirit.

### 4.2 Sub-track naming

Theme names only (section 6). No beginner/intermediate/advanced labels at launch (section 3.4).

### 4.3 Sign-off clip concept

2 to 4 seconds, same every episode: Aaron, deadpan and a little too pleased with himself, delivers a goofy "look what I can do" beat (gesture at the screen, tiny arms-out flourish, or a single silly physical bit) then smash-cut to the end card. Self-aware, dates him slightly on purpose, filmed once in a batch of 3 or 4 variants and reused. His own performance, no MADtv footage (section 4.1).

### 4.4 Visual identity direction

Base language: **personal-professional-editorial (No. 12 in `C:\twobirds\two-birds-portfolio\hal-stack\design\design-languages.md`)**, because the series is Aaron's personal brand surface and will live on the aaron-patzalek site. Applied to video:

- End cards and text overlays on warm cream `#faf9f5`, near-black ink, ONE warm coral-family accent for the theme tag and CTA.
- Serif display (Lora or Source Serif 4, OFL) for the episode title on the end card; Inter for captions and overlays. Self-hosted/OFL only.
- No gradients, no AI-default neon/glassmorphism, no stock-futuristic imagery. It should look like a well-set book page, not a tech channel.

## 5. Hook library

Openers for beat 1. Rotate; never invent claims to fit a hook. Bank of 15:

1. "Look what I can do: [result in five words]."
2. "My wife was [relatable frustration]. So I did this."
3. "This photo just became my family's calendar. Watch."
4. "I haven't sorted my own inbox since [month]."
5. "A robot did my chores last night. The boring digital ones."
6. "I made my phone ping me only when it actually matters."
7. "You know that [annoying task]? I don't do it anymore."
8. "It took me [honest small time] to never do this again."
9. "This is the laziest thing I've ever built. It works."
10. "I asked AI to interview my mother-in-law. Genuinely."
11. "Watch this spreadsheet-y problem disappear."
12. "I can't draw / code / design. Made this anyway."
13. "The dog is trained. Sort of. Here's the checklist that did it."
14. "Everyone talks about AI. Here's a boring, useful thing it did for me today."
15. "I built a thing that builds things. Stay with me."

Pattern rules: first person, plain words, a concrete noun in the first sentence, zero authority claims, self-deprecation welcome. Banned in hooks: "expert," "masterclass," "hack your," "10x," "you're doing it wrong."

Five themes. Public-facing labels are short and plain; "epic/story" never appears publicly. Corner-tag format: THEME NAME + episode number.

## 9. Staleness strategy (recommendation)
